## Safety comes first

Our workshop has machines that can seriously injure you in seconds. There are substances that make you ill, and a car with 600 volts. This is not meant to frighten you, but to give you respect.

The good news: almost all accidents can be avoided with a few clear rules. This chapter explains them to you step by step.

You can work through this chapter at any time, independently of other chapters. On each page you can only move on once you have solved the exercises correctly. At the end there is a quiz; after that you can download your certificate of completion as a PDF.

### The basic rules

- **The first commandment is common sense.** If something seems odd to you, you stop and ask.
- **Better too much protection than too little.** Fingers do not grow back well.
- **Check every tool with a quick look before using it:** Is the cable intact? Is everything secure? Is a protective cover missing?
- **If a device is damaged,** you unplug it, store it safely, secure it against being switched on again and report the damage.
- **You report every safety risk you discover** – even if it is not “yours”.

:::achtung Part of your safety instruction
This online instruction is **part of the safety instruction** of Einstein Motorsport. In addition, there are **machine-specific inductions on site** – on every machine you want to use.
:::

## Reading safety signs

There are many signs in the workshop. You do not have to memorise every single one. It is enough to understand **shape and colour** – then you know immediately what it is about:

| What it looks like | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Blue circle, white symbol | **Mandatory sign** | You **must** do this, e.g. wear safety glasses. |
| Red circle with diagonal bar | **Prohibition sign** | This is **prohibited**, e.g. gloves at the drill. |
| Yellow triangle, black border | **Warning sign** | **Caution**, there is a hazard here, e.g. electrical voltage. |
| White diamond with red border | **Hazard pictogram** (GHS) | The substance in the can or bottle is **hazardous**, e.g. corrosive. |
| Green sign | **Emergency sign** | There is **help** here: emergency exit, first-aid kit, assembly point. |
| Red sign, white symbol | **Fire safety sign** | There is something **against fire** here: fire extinguisher, fire alarm. |

## Protective equipment and clothing

Your personal protective equipment is the last thing standing between you and an injury. Which equipment you need depends on the work:

| Equipment | When? |
|---|---|
| **Safety glasses** | For all work where something can fly or splash: drilling, sanding, angle grinding, mixing resin. |
| **Hearing protection** | With loud machines, especially the angle grinder. |
| **Face protection (visor)** | In addition to safety glasses when using the angle grinder and when there are many sparks. |
| **Full-face respirator** (with face shield) | Always when carbon or aramid dust is produced. Safety glasses alone are not enough then – your face would be exposed to the dust. |
| **Full-body suit** | When working with carbon dust, so that the dust does not stay on your skin and clothing. |
| **Protective gloves** | When handling resins and chemicals – **but never on rotating tools**. |
| **Sturdy, closed shoes** | Always in the workshop. Falling parts and chips on the floor are everywhere. |

**Where do I find the protective equipment?** In the workshop there is a box with respirators and safety glasses. Gloves are provided by the team leads. The location changes again and again during the season – if you cannot find anything, you contact the responsible team lead **before starting work**.

### The glove rule

Gloves protect – but not everywhere. Remember the difference:

- **Resins and chemicals:** gloves **on**. They keep the substance away from your skin.
- **Rotating tools** (pillar drill, belt sander, angle grinder, cordless screwdriver, saws, lathe and milling machine): gloves **off**. If the material is caught by the rotating tool, your hand is pulled in with it – faster than you can let go.

![Pillar drill, belt sander, cordless screwdriver, jigsaw, circular saw and the prohibition sign “Gloves prohibited”](assets/bilder/sicherheit/rotierende-werkzeuge.webp "For all tools that rotate, the rule is: no gloves.")

### Clothing and hair

At rotating machines, anything loose becomes dangerous:

- **no loose clothing**, no open jackets, sleeves tight or rolled up
- **no scarves, cords, chains, rings or bracelets**
- when there are sparks (angle grinder, grinding) **clothing made of cotton or flame-resistant material** – synthetic fibres catch fire or melt onto your skin
- **tie long hair back** or tuck it under a cap

What do you do in this situation? You get feedback on every answer, including the wrong ones.

:::szenario From laminating to the drill

- [ ] Keep the gloves on – they protect you, after all > Dangerous: the gloves are meant for the resin. At the drill, the glove can be caught by the drill bit, and your hand is pulled in with it.
- [x] Take the gloves off properly, wash your hands, then drill without gloves and with safety glasses > Correct: the right protective equipment for every job. At the rotating tool that means: no gloves.
- [ ] Take off only one glove and hold the part with the other hand > Even one glove is enough to get caught. And workpieces at the drill are clamped, not held by hand.
:::

## What can happen: gloves, scarves and hair

The rules on the last page sound strict. Here you see why. Every example has happened like this in workshops before. A rotating spindle does not stop because you get a fright: it winds up several turns in one second, and its force is much greater than yours.

### Drilling with gloves

**The mistake:** You keep your gloves on because the part has sharp edges.

**What happens:** The drill bit catches the fabric and winds it up. The glove tightens around your fingers, the machine keeps pulling. You can no longer let go.

**The consequence:** Fingers are torn out of the hand, often **together with the tendon**, which is pulled out all the way from the forearm. Such avulsion injuries can usually not be reattached. On top of that come fractures and torn-off skin.

:::piktogramm A glove at the drill can cost you a finger. motiv=handschuh-bohrer groesse=gross
:::
### Scarf, cord or chain

**The mistake:** You wear a scarf, a hoodie with cords or a necklace at the machine.

**What happens:** The loose end gets to the spindle and is wound up. It pulls your neck towards the machine and strangles it.

**The consequence:** **Strangulation.** Unconsciousness within seconds, neck injuries, **death**. Nobody next to you is fast enough to stop it.

:::piktogramm A scarf at the machine is life-threatening. motiv=schal-maschine groesse=gross
:::
### Loose long hair

**The mistake:** You lean over the running machine with your hair loose.

**What happens:** Individual strands get caught on the spindle or drill chuck and are wound up in fractions of a second, with your head following.

**The consequence:** The **scalp is torn off** (scalping). That means many operations; bald patches and scars often remain forever.

:::piktogramm Hair is wound up in fractions of a second. motiv=haare-maschine groesse=gross
:::
### Rings and synthetic fibres

**A ring** can get caught on an edge or on the tool. It then tears skin and tissue off the finger, down to the bone. **Clothing made of synthetic fibres** catches fire from sparks or melts onto the skin. Such burns are deep and heal poorly.

Match each mistake to the consequence it risks.

:::zuordnen What is the consequence?
Drilling with gloves => Finger torn out with the tendon | The glove is wound up and pulls the hand with it.
Scarf at the machine => Strangulation | The end is wound up and strangles the neck.
Loose long hair => Scalp torn off | Strands are wound up in fractions of a second.
Synthetic fibres when using the angle grinder => Burns | Sparks set synthetic fibres on fire or melt them onto the skin.
:::

What do you do? You get feedback on every answer.

:::szenario A cold day in the workshop
It is winter. You are wearing a scarf and a hoodie with long cords and quickly want to drill a hole on the pillar drill.
- [ ] Tuck the scarf into the hoodie, that is enough > One loose end is enough for the spindle to catch the scarf. Then your neck is pulled towards the machine – strangulation.
- [x] Scarf off, cords out or hoodie off, hair tied back, jewellery off, then drill > Correct: nothing loose may hang down at rotating machines. Otherwise you risk strangulation, a torn-off scalp or torn-out fingers.
- [ ] Drill with gloves so that at least your hands stay warm > Gloves are prohibited at the drill. The drill bit winds them up and tears fingers out of the hand together with the tendon.
:::

## High voltage: 600 volts – hands off

Our race car is electric. Its high-voltage system operates at **600 volts**. For comparison: a wall socket has 230 volts. A single mistake can be **fatal** – and you cannot tell by looking at a car whether it is live.

The high-voltage system includes the battery, the power electronics, the motors and all the cables in between. In the rules, this part is called the Tractive System, **TS** for short.

![Orange high-voltage cable with a stripped copper end](assets/bilder/sicherheit/hv-kabel.webp "You can recognise high voltage by the signal colour orange.")

### The rules for everyone

- **Only Team HV works on the high-voltage system** – and only members with the right training. For everyone else: **hands off.**
- **Orange means high voltage.** All high-voltage cables are orange. You do not touch orange cables, connectors and components.
- **You do not enter the HV trailer.**
- **You do not plug in or unplug anything without asking first** – not even “just for a moment”.
- **When the car is being commissioned,** the workshop is closed off with **barrier tape**. You do not enter it then.
- **When the car is live, you never stand in front of or behind the car** – and never next to the wheels. The car accelerates from 0 to 100 km/h in less than 2.5 seconds. If it suddenly drives off, you cannot get away. Rotating wheels can also fling parts away or come loose. The only exception is the authorised electricians of Team HV.

:::info Why so strict?
The Formula Student rules require that an ESO (Electrical System Officer) is responsible for work on the high-voltage system. This person needs a high-voltage qualification with theory and practice. Only an ESO may declare the car “electrically safe”.
:::

**Who is responsible?** The ESOs are appointed within the team and are known. If you do not know whom to ask, you contact the **project lead**.

How do you decide? There is an explanation for every answer.

:::szenario A loose connector
You are helping to tidy up next to the car. You notice that an orange connector looks only half plugged in. The car is not in operation at the moment.
- [ ] Quickly push the connector in – it takes two seconds > No: orange means high voltage. Even a switched-off car can carry dangerous voltage. You do not touch it.
- [ ] Take a photo and ask in the chat later > Reporting is good, but a loose high-voltage connector is urgent. Team HV must know immediately.
- [x] Touch nothing, keep your distance and inform Team HV immediately > Correct: you report what you found immediately and leave the rest to the trained people.
:::

## What can happen: 600 volts

You cannot see, hear or smell electricity. At 600 volts there is no “small shock” as a warning.

### Current through the body

**The mistake:** You touch an orange cable or a connector “because the car is switched off anyway”.

**What happens:** The current flows through your body, for example from your hand to your feet, straight through your chest.

**The consequence:** The heart loses its rhythm (**ventricular fibrillation**). That is cardiac arrest: without resuscitation you are dead within a few minutes. Along the current path, tissue burns **inside**, even if hardly anything can be seen on the outside. Cardiac arrhythmias can still occur **hours later**. That is why a doctor must examine the person after every electrical accident.

:::piktogramm Current through the chest throws the heart out of rhythm. motiv=hv-koerper groesse=gross
:::
### Electric arc

**The mistake:** A spanner or another metal part touches two high-voltage poles at the same time, for example on the battery.

**What happens:** An **electric arc** forms: a flash of several thousand degrees. Metal vaporises and molten metal sprays around.

**The consequence:** **Severe burns** to hands and face, even without current flowing through you. The glaring light can damage the eyes.

:::piktogramm An electric arc burns you without you touching the cable. motiv=hv-lichtbogen groesse=gross
:::
### The car drives off

When the car is live, it can move off without warning, reaching 100 km/h in less than 2.5 seconds. Anyone standing in front of or behind it will be **run over or crushed**. That is why the rule is: never in front of, behind or next to a live car.

Metal part touches two high-voltage poles => Severe burns | An electric arc of several thousand degrees forms.
Standing in front of a live car => Run over or crushed | The car can move off without warning, reaching 100 km/h in under 2.5 seconds.
:::

## The TSAL: red means hands off

How can you tell whether the high-voltage system is currently active? For this, every electric car in Formula Student has a warning light: the TSAL (Tractive System Active Light). It is located **at the top of the roll hoop behind the driver’s seat** and is so bright that you can see it even in sunlight.

The rules specify exactly when which colour lights up:

| The TSAL … | This means | You … |
|---|---|---|
| **flashes red** | The high-voltage system is **active**: more than 60 volts are present. | **Hands off the race car.** Keep your distance. |
| **is steady green** | The high-voltage system is **off** and disconnected from the battery. | Still only work on the car if Team HV has given clearance. |
| **is not lit at all** | The low-voltage on-board system is also off. Without low voltage, the battery cannot be active in our car. | The car is **generally safe** – unless someone from Team HV says otherwise. |
| **red and green at the same time** | A **fault**. | Treat it as active: **hands off** and inform Team HV immediately. |

Remember: **If the TSAL flashes red or shows red and green at the same time, the high-voltage system is considered active.** That is what the rules say.

If the high-voltage system is active, the following also applies: **never stand in front of, behind or next to the car** – not even for a short moment. The car can drive off without warning, and rotating wheels can fling parts away.

### Other signals on the car

- **The ready-to-drive sound:** Before the motors respond to the accelerator pedal, the car plays a loud signal tone (1 to 3 seconds). From then on the car is ready to drive – and it can **drive off immediately**. Electric cars are almost silent. At the latest now, you are away from the area in front of, behind and next to the car.
- **The shutdown buttons:** The car has three emergency shutdown buttons: one each on the left and right behind the driver’s head and one in the cockpit. One press switches off the high-voltage system. They are there for emergencies.
- **Barrier tape and sign:** When Team HV is working on the high-voltage system, the car is cordoned off with barrier tape. A sign shows which ESO is responsible. **Only this ESO** may remove the tape and sign again.

Which observation requires which response? Match them; afterwards you see whether you are right, and why.

:::zuordnen What do you do if …
the TSAL flashes red => Hands off – considered active | Flashing red means: high voltage is active. Do not touch, keep your distance.
the TSAL is not lit at all => Generally safe – unless Team HV says otherwise | Without light, the low-voltage on-board system is also off. Without low voltage, the battery cannot be active.
the TSAL shows red and green at the same time => Hands off – considered active | Both colours at the same time are a fault. A fault is always treated as “active”.
the TSAL is steady green => Only with clearance from Team HV | Green means: high voltage is off. You still only work on the car once Team HV allows it.
the ready-to-drive sound plays => Away from the area in front of, behind and next to the car | From the sound onwards, the motors respond to the accelerator pedal – the car can drive off immediately.
:::

## Low voltage: only after induction

Besides the high-voltage system, the car has a second on-board system: the low-voltage system, **LV** for short. It supplies control units, sensors, lights, pumps and fans. In our car it operates at **24 volts**, and the cables are **never orange**.

But “low” does not mean “harmless”:

- In a **short circuit**, so much current can flow that cables glow, melt or start a **fire**.
- A wrongly connected plug can **destroy control units** that the team has worked on for months.
- Batteries in the low-voltage system are usually **lithium batteries** – if damaged, they can catch fire.

![Green circuit board with resistors, capacitors and plug contacts](assets/bilder/sicherheit/platine.webp "Electronics in the low-voltage system: sensitive and expensive.")

### The rules

- You work on the low-voltage system **only after an induction by the Electrics team**.
- **If you have no idea: hands off** – and ask.
- **Do not plug in or unplug anything** without asking first.
- **You report damage immediately**, for example a cable where the copper is already visible.

**Who gives the induction?** The induction for the low-voltage system is given by the **Electrics Team Lead**.

**Damaged lithium batteries** are a special case. They can go into thermal runaway: they then keep heating up, burn violently and are very hard to extinguish. A warning sign is a **swollen** battery.

What do you do? There is an explanation for every answer.

:::szenario A swollen battery

- [ ] Throw it in the normal rubbish > Damaged lithium batteries can ignite – in the rubbish this is particularly dangerous.
- [ ] Charge it briefly to check whether it still works > Charging is the most dangerous thing you can do with a damaged battery. It can go into runaway while charging.
- [x] Do not touch it any further, keep your distance and inform the Electrics team immediately > Correct: a swollen battery is damaged. The Electrics team puts it in a safe container and has it disposed of properly.
:::

## What can happen: short circuit and battery fire

You hardly feel 24 volts on your skin. What is dangerous here is not the voltage, but the **current** that a battery delivers in a short circuit.

### Ring or watch on the battery

**The mistake:** You work on a battery wearing a ring, watch or bracelet, and the metal touches both poles.

**What happens:** A huge current flows through the metal. The ring becomes **red-hot within a few seconds**, and you cannot get it off your finger fast enough.

**The consequence:** **Burns down to the bone**, all around the finger. That is why the rule is: jewellery off before you work on electrics.

:::piktogramm A ring that touches both poles becomes red-hot in seconds. motiv=lv-ring groesse=gross
:::
### A damaged battery

**The mistake:** A swollen or dropped lithium battery continues to be used or is even charged.

**What happens:** The battery goes into thermal runaway: it keeps heating itself up until it burns. Water hardly extinguishes it, and it can also reignite.

**The consequence:** **Fire** in the workshop and **toxic gases**, including hydrogen fluoride, which corrodes the airways and lungs.

:::piktogramm A burning lithium battery releases toxic gases. motiv=akku-brand groesse=gross
:::

Charging a swollen lithium battery => Fire and toxic gases | The battery can go into thermal runaway and release hydrogen fluoride.
:::

## Workshop rules

A few basic rules apply to all machines in the workshop – including for experienced members:

1. **You only use machines you have been inducted on.** The induction is given by the Manufacturing team or by a person designated for this by the project lead. It is documented.
2. **Milling machine and lathe only with a separate induction.** The general workshop instruction is not enough for these. These machines are so dangerous that you receive a separate, detailed induction.
3. **You never work alone in the workshop.** If something happens, someone must be there to help and make the emergency call.
4. **A visual inspection before every use:** cables, plugs, protective covers, tools.
5. **If a device is damaged, it is taken out of service** – so that nobody else uses it.

**Whom do you ask?** If you do not know who can give you an induction, you contact the **project lead**.

:::info
Unsure whether you are allowed to use a machine? Then you are not – ask first. Nobody will hold it against you.
:::

What exactly do you do with a damaged device? There are four steps. The first is always: power off. Then the device is moved out of the way so that nobody trips over it or uses it by mistake. After that, you make sure it is not switched on again – for example with a note saying “Defective – do not use” on the plug. Finally, you report the damage – and say **where the device is now**. Otherwise the next person will search for it or not find it again.

## Pillar drill: fingers at risk

The pillar drill looks harmless. But it is one of the machines on which **fingers are lost** most often in workshops. The drill bit turns with so much force that it pulls in anything it catches: a glove, a sleeve, hair – and the hand inside.

:::achtung Gloves are prohibited at the pillar drill
No gloves – not even with sharp-edged parts. A glove can get caught on the drill bit or the spindle. Then the hand is pulled into the machine, and you can no longer let go. The result is the most severe injuries, up to the loss of fingers.
:::

:::achtung Always secure the workpiece and the vice
A workpiece that breaks loose spins with full force and can strike through the working area like a projectile. Therefore: clamp the workpiece firmly in the vice **and** secure the vice to the table with a bolt. A loose vice is life-threatening.
:::

![Pillar drill](assets/bilder/sicherheit/staenderbohrmaschine.webp "The pillar drill: harmless in appearance, dangerous if you are careless.")

### How to work safely

- Wear **safety glasses**, **no gloves**, hair tied back, sleeves tight, no jewellery.
- **Always clamp the workpiece**, for example in the machine vice. Never hold it by hand – if the drill bit jams, it tears the workpiece along with it.
- Additionally secure **long workpieces** (bars, tubes, profiles) against rotating, for example with a stop on the column. Never stand in the area where the long end could swing around.
- **The vice itself must be secured to the machine table with a bolt.** Otherwise it can break loose together with the workpiece, fly around the working area and hit you – **danger to life**.
- **Remove the chuck key** before you switch on. A forgotten key is flung away when the machine is switched on.
- Set the **speed** to suit the drill bit and the material. Ask if you do not know it.
- **Never remove chips by hand.** They are sharp and hot. Switch off the machine, wait until it has stopped, then remove them with a brush or chip hook.
- **Never brake** the machine **by hand.** Wait until it stops by itself.

What do you do? You get feedback on every answer.

:::szenario Quickly drilling a hole
You want to drill a hole in a small aluminium part on the pillar drill. The part has sharp edges, so you are still wearing your work gloves.
- [ ] Keep the gloves on – they protect against the sharp edges > Dangerous: the glove can be caught by the drill bit or spindle. Then the hand is pulled in with it.
- [x] Take off the gloves, clamp the workpiece in the vice, put on safety glasses > Correct: the clamped workpiece protects your hands better than any glove – and the safety glasses protect your eyes from chips.
- [ ] Hold the part by hand, it will only take a moment > The workpiece can jam and spin with the drill. With a small part, your hand is then right at the drill bit. Always clamp it.

## Using the belt sander safely

On the belt sander, a sanding belt runs quickly over two rollers. You use it to deburr edges or grind small parts into shape. The hazards: the belt can **grind away skin in seconds**, sparks and grinding dust fly **into your face**, and the workpiece gets **hot**.

![Grinding machine with grinding wheel on the left and sanding belt on the right](assets/bilder/sicherheit/bandschleifer.webp "Combined grinding machine with sanding belt (example image)")

### Before sanding

- Wear **safety glasses**. No gloves, hair tied back, sleeves tight.
- **Check the belt:** Is it intact, not torn, not frayed, correctly tensioned, and does it run centrally?
- **Adjust the work rest:** The work rest is set as close as possible to the belt. If the gap is too large, the workpiece – or your finger – can be pulled in.
- **Switch on the extraction** if there is one.
- Remove anything **combustible** from the surroundings: rags, paper, solvents.

### While sanding

- Switch on the machine and wait until the belt has reached **full speed**.
- **Place the workpiece on the work rest** and hold it with both hands. Guide it to the belt with **light pressure** – do not force it.
- **Move the workpiece back and forth** so that the belt wears evenly.
- **Keep your fingers away from the belt.** You do not sand very small parts freehand – ask for a suitable holder.
- **Caution, hot!** Let the workpiece cool down before you touch it at the sanded area.
- Do not sand **aluminium and steel** on the same belt. Fine aluminium dust can react dangerously with steel sparks. Ask which belt is for which material.
- **Carbon is never sanded here** (more on this in the section on carbon dust).

### After sanding

- Switch off and **wait until the belt has stopped.** Never brake it with your hand or the workpiece.
- Remove grinding dust with a brush or vacuum cleaner, not by hand or by blowing.
- Report worn or damaged belts.

In what order do you work on the belt sander? Move the steps to the correct position and then check your solution.

:::

**The consequence:** Skin, fingernail and tissue are **ground away down to the bone in seconds**. Such wounds heal slowly and often leave a shortened or numb finger.

- **Touching the workpiece directly after sanding:** Metal quickly heats up to over 100 °C during sanding. The result is **burn blisters**.
- **Sanding with a torn belt:** If the belt snaps, it whips around and can hit you on the hand or face.

## Angle grinder: protection, disc and fire safety

The “Flex” is properly called an angle grinder. Its disc rotates up to **200 times per second**. It is loud, produces a lot of sparks – and a broken disc can **burst**. The fragments then fly through the room like projectiles.

:::achtung Your eyesight is at stake
A spark, a splinter or a piece of cutting disc in your eye – and your eyesight can be lost forever. Fragments of a burst disc can also cause deep cuts and life-threatening injuries. That is why there is no exception for protective equipment at the angle grinder.
:::

### Your protective equipment when using the angle grinder

- **Hearing protection** – the angle grinder is so loud that it damages your hearing.
- **Safety glasses**, ideally with a **face shield** (visor) in addition.
- **No gloves** – the angle grinder is also a rotating tool.
- **Clothing made of cotton or flame-resistant material.** Sparks ignite synthetic fibres or melt them onto the skin – you could set yourself on fire.

### Checking the machine

- The **guard must be fitted to the angle grinder.** It sits between the disc and you. Without a guard, no grinding.
- **Check the disc:** Does it have cracks or chipped areas, or is it bent? Then it is not used.
- **Check the expiry date:** Cutting discs become brittle over time. The metal ring shows the date until which they may be used. Expired discs are discarded.
- The disc must **match the machine**: diameter and permitted speed must be correct.
- To change the disc, **pull the plug** or remove the battery.

![Two damaged grinding discs with large chipped areas](assets/bilder/sicherheit/trennscheiben-beschaedigt.webp "A disc must never look like this: chipped areas and cracks.")

![Cutting disc, a red arrow points to the expiry date on the metal ring](assets/bilder/sicherheit/trennscheibe-verfallsdatum.webp "The expiry date is on the metal ring of the disc.")

### Using the angle grinder safely

- **Clamp the workpiece firmly.**
- **Always hold** the angle grinder **with both hands**, by the main handle and the auxiliary handle.
- **Do not stand in the plane of the disc:** if it bursts, the pieces fly exactly in this direction.
- Keep the **cable** away from the disc.
- After switching off, **wait until the disc has stopped** before putting the tool down.

### Fire safety: angle grinding only outside the workshop

:::achtung Angle grinding only outside the workshop – never inside the workshop
The sparks from an angle grinder fly several metres and are hot enough to ignite rags, paper, resin residues or solvents. There is too much of these in the workshop. That is why angle grinding is only done outside the workshop.
:::

- Before angle grinding: **remove anything combustible** and direct the sparks so that they do not hit anyone.
- Have a **fire extinguisher** within reach. Fire extinguishers are located **in the workshop** and **at the Push Pull Bar** – check which one is closer before you start grinding.
- After angle grinding: **check** the area for **smouldering embers** for a few more minutes.

**What happens:** The disc bursts. The fragments fly at almost 300 km/h exactly where you are standing.

**The consequence:** **Lost eyes**, deep cuts to the face and **neck**. A splinter in the carotid artery is **fatal**.

## Carbon dust: full-body suit and respiratory protection

Our car consists largely of fibre composite: carbon (carbon fibre) and aramid. A finished component is harmless. It becomes dangerous when you **sand, drill, saw or mill** it: this produces fine dust.

**Carbon and aramid dusts are suspected of causing cancer.** During machining, the fibres splinter into tiny fragments that get deep into the lungs and stay there. They also irritate the skin, eyes and airways and itch intensely. What exactly this means, you can read after this page.

:::achtung Only machine carbon and aramid outside
Sanding, drilling and sawing carbon and aramid is done **outside the workshop – never inside the workshop.** The only exception is the milling machine: if carbon is milled there, then only with respiratory protection and an industrial vacuum cleaner suitable for carbon dust. And the rule always applies: **full-face respirator and full-body suit.**
:::

### Your protective equipment for carbon dust

- **Full-face respirator** with particle filter that covers the whole face. Safety glasses alone are not enough – otherwise your face is exposed to the dust.
- **Full-body suit** with hood (disposable coverall). This way the dust does not stay on your skin and clothing – and you do not carry it home.
- If possible: **extraction** directly at the point where the dust is produced.

### After work

- Pick up dust with a **suitable industrial vacuum cleaner**. **Never blow it off with compressed air or sweep it dry** – that really stirs up the dust.
- **Take off the suit carefully** (roll it inside out) and **throw it away** – it is not reused. You only take off the mask after that.
- **Wash your hands and face.** Do not eat or drink near carbon dust.

## What carbon and aramid dust do to the body

A cut in your finger hurts immediately. Fibre dust does not. You breathe it in, hardly notice anything, and the consequences may only appear **decades later**.

### Tiny needles in the lungs

When sanding, drilling and sawing, the fibres break. This produces fragments so small that they get into the **alveoli**, the deepest part of the lungs. Some of them have exactly the shape known from **asbestos**: longer than 5 micrometres, thinner than 3 micrometres, i.e. long, thin and pointed. Experts call them **WHO fibres**. For comparison: a hair is about 20 times as thick.

The body **hardly breaks down** carbon. Whatever reaches the lungs may stay there forever.

:::piktogramm Fibre fragments get into the alveoli. motiv=carbon-lunge groesse=gross
:::
### The suspicion of cancer

- Such carbon fibre fragments are **suspected of causing cancer**. The German Social Accident Insurance (DGUV) classifies them in **category 2**: suspected of being carcinogenic.
- In the laboratory, the fragments cause **inflammation, cell death and damage to the genetic material** in lung cells.
- In the case of asbestos, exactly such fibres caused **lung cancer** and **mesothelioma**, a cancer of the pleura. Both diseases appeared **20 to 40 years** after inhalation and were then usually no longer curable.
- **Aramid** frays during machining into extremely fine fibrils, which are also respirable. In long-term experiments on rats, the lung tissue scarred (**fibrosis**). Whether aramid dust causes cancer has not been clarified.

**To be honest:** Whether carbon and aramid dust cause cancer in humans has not yet been conclusively researched. That is exactly the problem. With asbestos, too, people did not know for decades, and even today well over a thousand people die from it in Germany every year. You do not want to take that chance. That is why we treat fibre dust as if it were definitely carcinogenic.

### What else happens

- **Skin:** The fibre fragments prick the skin. The result is severe **itching**, redness and skin inflammation. Resin residues in the dust can trigger **allergic eczema**.
- **Eyes and airways:** burning, watering eyes, coughing, irritated bronchi.
- **Dust travels with you:** Without a suit, you carry the dust home on your clothes and hair, to your family, into your bed.
- **Dust conducts electricity:** Carbon dust in electronics causes **short circuits**, in the car too.
- **Dust burns:** Carbon dust stirred up in the air is combustible and can even explode.

:::piktogramm Without a suit, you carry the dust all the way home. motiv=carbon-zuhause groesse=gross
:::

## Resins and other hazardous substances

Carbon components are made with **resin** (usually epoxy resin) and hardener. Both are hazardous substances. The insidious part: resin can **trigger allergies that last a lifetime.** Once you are allergic, you are often never allowed to work with resin again.

:::achtung Only touch resin with the right gloves
For resins you use **nitrile gloves** – no latex and no fabric gloves, they let the resin through. If a glove is torn or covered in resin, you change it immediately.
:::

### How to work safely with resin

- Wear **nitrile gloves**, **safety glasses** and long sleeves.
- Ensure **good ventilation**.
- **Take off gloves properly:** do not touch the outside with bare skin.
- **Resin on the skin:** wash it off immediately with soap and water – never with solvent, which drives the resin deeper into the skin.
- **Resin in the eye:** rinse immediately with water for at least 10 minutes and then see a doctor.
- There is a **safety data sheet** for every hazardous substance. Read it before you work with the substance.

**Where are the safety data sheets, and where do resin residues go?** Ask your **team lead** – before you work with the substance.

### Caution, hot: mixing resin correctly

Resin and hardener react with each other – this produces **heat**. If you mix too much at once, the mixture can **become very hot, smoke and even start to burn.**

- Only mix as much as you really need within the working time.
- Use **suitable mixing containers** – no small, thin plastic cups. They simply melt with larger quantities.
- Do not leave mixed resin standing in a lump; let residues cure spread out flat.
- Do not place anything combustible directly next to the mixing container.

### Storing hazardous substances correctly

Hazardous substances stay **in their original packaging** or in a container labelled in exactly the same way. **Never decant them into drinks bottles** – otherwise someone will drink from them by mistake.

![Poster: a figure pours a liquid from a canister with hazard pictograms into a drinks bottle, above it the heading “Never put hazardous substances in drinks bottles!”](assets/bilder/sicherheit/plakat-getraenkeflaschen.webp "Hazardous substances never belong in drinks bottles. (Poster: Suva)")

## What can happen: resin and hazardous substances

Resin smells harmless and feels like honey. The consequences are serious nonetheless.

### Resin on the skin

**The mistake:** You touch resin without nitrile gloves or wipe it off with solvent.

**What happens:** Your immune system learns to recognise the resin as an enemy (sensitisation). Often you do not notice this at all the first time.

**The consequence:** An **allergy that lasts a lifetime**: red, weeping, itchy skin, even with the smallest amounts or even from vapours in the air. Once you are allergic, you are often **never allowed to work with resin again**, not even later in your job.

:::piktogramm A resin allergy lasts a lifetime. motiv=harz-haut groesse=gross
:::
### Too much resin at once

**The mistake:** You mix a large cup of resin so that it is enough for everything, and leave it standing.

**What happens:** Resin and hardener generate heat, and in a large lump it cannot escape. The mixture becomes **well over 100 °C** hot, the cup melts, it **gives off toxic smoke** and can **catch fire**.

**The consequence:** Burns, irritation of the airways from the smoke and a fire in the middle of the workshop.

:::piktogramm Too much mixed resin can ignite by itself. motiv=harz-hitze groesse=gross
:::
### Hazardous substance in a drinks bottle

**The mistake:** You pour some solvent or hardener into an empty water bottle because nothing else is available at the moment.

**What happens:** Someone else is thirsty, sees the bottle and drinks.

**The consequence:** **Poisoning**, chemical burns to the mouth and oesophagus. Some substances permanently damage internal organs.

:::piktogramm Hazardous substances never belong in drinks bottles. motiv=getraenkeflasche groesse=gross

## When something happens

In an emergency, every second counts. That is why the rule **before every job** is: check where the nearest **fire extinguisher** and the nearest **first-aid kit** are – depending on where you are working.

- The workshop’s **first-aid kit** is **at the workshop entrance in the orange case**.
- **Fire extinguishers** are located **in the workshop** and **at the Push Pull Bar**.
- The **escape route** to the exit always stays clear – do not put anything in front of it.

**In an emergency:**

1. Stay calm, do not put yourself in danger.
2. Switch off the machine using the emergency stop. On the race car: press a shutdown button if you can reach it without danger, and shout loudly for Team HV.
3. Call the emergency number **112** – Where did it happen? What happened? How many injured? What injuries? Wait for questions.
4. **Have someone guide the ambulance:** If possible, get a second person to wait for the ambulance at the university entrance and lead it to the right place. Tip: on Google Maps, the workshop can be found under **“Einstein Motorsport”** – you can tell the emergency services this.
5. Give first aid – as well as you can. We do not have designated first aiders (see below).
6. Report the accident to the **project lead** – even minor injuries are documented.

:::achtung In the event of an electrical accident
If someone is stuck to a live source, **never touch the person with your hand** – otherwise the current will also flow through you. First switch off the power (emergency stop, shutdown button). If that is not possible, push the person away with a **broom or another non-conductive object**. Then immediately call Team HV and 112. After every electrical accident, the person affected must be examined by a doctor, even if they feel fine.
:::

:::info Why we do not have designated first aiders
Einstein Motorsport is a club: hardly anyone is in the workshop permanently and on a predictable schedule. That is why there cannot be designated first aiders. All the more important: in an emergency, **call 112 immediately** – and never work alone in the workshop.
:::

No certificate and no induction on the machine means no work in the workshop – not even “just quickly”. The onboarding certificate at the end of the entire course is **not** proof of the safety instruction.

## Summary

- This online instruction is **part of your safety instruction**. Hand in the certificate PDF to the project lead; **machine-specific inductions** are given in addition on site.
- **Safety signs:** blue = mandatory, red with a diagonal bar = prohibited, yellow = warning, red diamond = hazardous substance.
- **High voltage (600 V):** Only Team HV with training. Orange means high voltage – hands off. When the car is live: **never in front of, behind or next to the car.**
- **TSAL:** Flashing red, or red and green at the same time = **hands off the race car.** Green = high voltage off, work only with clearance. Dark = generally safe, unless Team HV says otherwise.
- **Low voltage (24 V):** only after an induction by the Electrics Team Lead. No idea? Hands off.
- **Rotating tools:** no gloves, no scarf, no jewellery, hair tied back, safety glasses. Otherwise you risk torn-out fingers, strangulation or a torn-off scalp. At the **pillar drill**, clamp the workpiece and bolt down the vice.
- **Milling machine and lathe** only with a separate induction. Don’t know whom to contact? Project lead.
- **Angle grinder:** hearing protection, safety glasses or visor, guard, clothing made of cotton or flame-resistant material – and **only outside the workshop**. Your eyes cannot be replaced.
- **Carbon and aramid:** Fibre dust gets into the alveoli and is **suspected of causing cancer**, often only after decades. Only machine outside, always a **full-face respirator** and full-body suit. **Resin:** nitrile gloves, only mix small amounts in suitable containers.
- **Preparation and tidying up are mandatory.** In an emergency: switch off the power, **112**, have someone guide the ambulance, first aid (orange case at the entrance), report the accident.
